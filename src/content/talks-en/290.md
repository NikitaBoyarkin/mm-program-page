The use of AI analysts is becoming a mass trend, but there is almost nothing to verify their work on real data with: without a manual duplicate query, the correctness of a calculation is not confirmed. So you can approach from the other side — not "what the agent can do", but "what needs to be done with the task, the data and the scaffolding so that a correct answer becomes more likely".

The result is a framework of eight steps from statement to accepted result, and at each step there is a check and a typical failure that occurs regardless of model and domain.

A live analytical backlog is laid out by image of the result - what appears when a task is closed: a number, an export, a dashboard, a pipeline cell, an edit to a report, a study, an agreement on a metric definition, an A/B test.

The first conclusion: transferability is determined not by the complexity of the task, but by whether the decisions it requires are declared. An undeclared decision will still be made: by a layer, a mart or a model, each time anew. And declaring is not enough. Between "we described this" and "the agent sees this" lies the scaffolding, and it loses: a significant part of the decisions recorded in model comments and tests does not reach the agent's context, and including a layer with table descriptions in one of the measurements actually narrowed the input.

Hence the second conclusion, less obvious: the answer is not the only outcome of the work.

An agent can stop and list what is missing, and each non-answer has its own party to blame: the framing of the question, the absence of an object in the data, the semantic layer, or the discipline of recording results.

It turns out that not everything is fixed by prompt and context.

Work must be accepted by image of the result: a number has its own truth, an export has another — a set of rows and a control aggregate, a study has none at all, and there the stability of the conclusion is checked, not correctness.

The talk examines several techniques with measured boundaries: comparison with a known answer, decomposition of a number into summands, checking that nothing extra was affected, addition in two ways, reading what the agent decided itself, recalculation with a different decision.

Attendees will get a sequence of actions that raises the share of correct answers, and a way to check each step for themselves: whether the declared decisions reach the agent, what changes in the input when a layer is enabled, and what to accept each image of the result with.

And separately - how to check your own check.
