Mind was not a predetermined goal of evolution — human intelligence emerged and developed not only inside the brain: tools preserved ways of acting, language transmitted experience, social groups accumulated knowledge, and writing and digital technologies created humanity's external memory.

One of the main amplifiers of human intelligence was the transmission of knowledge — language, imitation, teaching and a long childhood made cumulative culture possible. Human history is the history of external carriers of mind, and if earlier tools mostly stored and retrieved knowledge, generative systems are starting to assemble answers, offer explanations and take part in decision-making.

Stanislav Drobyshevsky traces this trajectory from stone technologies and the origin of speech to writing, digital data and generative models. Why was intelligence not an inevitable goal of evolution? How did cooperation help sapiens turn individual experience into culture? Which abilities do we gain and lose by handing memory and thinking over to external tools? And what can anthropology really say about humans in the age of AI?

This is a lecture about how the biological brain created an external world of knowledge and how the artificial information environment affects it, when AI changes the cultural environment faster than human biology
